The harbour was quiet the night the lanterns were lit, and Mira counted them twice because counting was the only thing that still made the fog feel like weather and not a warning. Eight along the eastern wall. Eight along the western wall. She had kept the lights for eleven years, and in that time she had learned the sound of every rope in the mast yard, the way the tide came in like a slow argument, and the particular silence that followed a boat that did not come back.

By midnight the fog had risen past the low wall and the lanterns burned inside it like coins at the bottom of a well. Mira wrote the hour in the log, then the wind, then the word she never wrote: missing. The bell rang once at two in the morning. There was no ship to ring it, and no hand on the rope that she could see, so she took the long lamp from its hook and walked the eastern wall from end to end, saying the names of the boats as she passed them.

The long lamp was older than the harbour. Its glass was thick and green at the edges, and when she lifted the shutter the beam went out over the water and did not stop where the fog began; it went into the fog and stayed there, steady, as though the fog had agreed to hold it. That was the whole of her work, in the end. Not saving anyone. Standing where the light could be seen, so that anyone still out there had one thing that was not the dark.
